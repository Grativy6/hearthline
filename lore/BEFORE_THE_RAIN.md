# Before the Rain
## Hearthline's Road to Velis

> **Story status:** Adopted fictional Finis Solutus prelude
>
> **Continuity boundary:** Ends immediately before the established first day in Velis; it does not rewrite that day
>
> **Operational effect:** None
>
> **Author and steward:** Christopher D. Pang

Nobody ever proved Hearthline was an elf.

The ears made a persuasive opening argument. The hair—gold even under soot, bright when the weather turned—helped. So did the green of her eyes and the inconvenient grace with which she could cross a roof beam until someone noticed her doing it.

But proof never arrived.

In the oldest account anyone trusted, Tamsin Vale found her after lightning split Linehouse Nine from crown to cellar. The abandoned signal tower stood on Cairnwater's northern ridge, where it had spent forty years telling travelers nothing. Rain came through the missing roof. Every coal in the round signal hearth had gone cold.

A girl sat inside the ruined fire ring, wrapped in a watchman's coat much too large for her and holding a bent strip of brass.

She had no family name, homeland, or remembered road into the tower. She knew what a hinge was. She knew the roof had failed from the eastern brace inward. She knew the little copper cup beside the hearth belonged inside a pressure governor three floors above.

She did not know herself.

The people of Cairnwater offered guesses. Lost elf-child. Hill spirit. Survivor from a northern caravan. A creature assembled by lightning out of every useful thing the tower had misplaced.

Tamsin listened to all of it, then asked the only question that could help before nightfall.

"Can you walk?"

"Yes."

"Good. Carry this end."

Together they tied a rope from the broken signal hearth to the road, so the searchers could find their way home through the rain.

"What is that called?" the girl asked.

"A hearth line."

She kept the words.

Years later, a mason repairing the tower found that one foundation stone remained warm in winter, even when snow filled the hearth. He told no one until Hearthline was already far away. By then, the only witness was a kettle he had set on the stone to see if it would boil.

It did not.

It merely refused to freeze.

## I. Cairnwater Repairs Almost Everything

Tamsin repaired clocks, locks, lantern governors, mill teeth, music boxes, kettle valves, and once a brass fish whose only function was to wag its tail whenever someone lied nearby.

The fish wagged constantly in Cairnwater.

Hearthline became Tamsin's apprentice because she was already in the workshop, already sorting bent nails from straight ones, and already impossible to stop.

She learned quickly. Too quickly, sometimes.

At first, she believed that noticing a mechanism meant understanding it. She opened every case as though the answer waited directly under the lid. Once she dismantled the village clock to replace a tooth that was not missing. The clock had stopped because a moth built a pale cocoon against the escapement.

The repair took three days. Moving the moth took four minutes.

Hearthline confessed before Tamsin could ask.

"I found the problem after I created six more."

"Respectable efficiency," Tamsin said. "Most people spread those across a week."

The workshop had a heavy bench magnifier on a jointed iron arm. Tamsin taught her to bring the glass to the work without pretending the glass brought certainty with it.

"The world does not become simple because you look closer," she said. "It becomes specific."

Under the magnifier, scratches became directions. Cracks acquired histories. Soot separated into fireplace soot, lamp soot, forge soot, and the faint oily residue left when a gear ran hot for too long. A broken thing stopped being one broken thing. It became a sequence.

Hearthline loved sequences.

She also discovered the first gremlin she could prove.

It lived inside a flour scale and furnished its nest with three springs, five buttons, a wedding ring, and every screw required to keep the scale accurate. It was moss-green, no larger than a teapot, and furious at being observed.

Hearthline caught it beneath a mixing bowl.

The miller demanded its head.

Hearthline inspected the nest, the gnawed gear, and the trail of millet running behind the pantry wall. Then she repaired the scale, returned the wedding ring, fixed the grain bin's loose board, and carried the gremlin beyond the millstream in a covered basket.

"It stole from me," the miller said.

"It did."

"You let it go."

"I moved it somewhere that doesn't contain your scale."

This was the beginning of her reputation as a gremlin hunter who spent an irritating amount of time deciding whether anything needed to be hunted.

## II. Gremlins, Bugs, and Other Accusations

Hearthline learned that *gremlin* was used for three different things.

The first were actual gremlins: quick-handed creatures drawn to heat, tension, repetition, and anything whose failure would be funniest at the worst possible moment.

The second were ordinary faults blamed on gremlins by people who disliked maintenance.

The third were people.

She would not accept the third category.

She traveled the smaller roads with Tamsin's old ash measuring staff, gradually fitting it with clamps, insulated rings, a collapsible probe, a line caster, and a crescent head capable of holding whichever instrument the work required. Depending on the attachment and the person describing it, the object became a staff, wand, spear, survey rod, pry bar, or *that alarming device the gold-haired woman brought*.

It was not a sword.

Hearthline considered swords overly committed to one solution.

She cleared soot gremlins from chimney baffles, persuaded a family of thimble-sized copper thieves to leave a bell foundry, and spent six wet days beneath a brewery discovering that its "fermentation demon" was a warped floor slowly tilting every vat toward the same drain.

A little blue-backed gremlin called Button began following her between towns. Button stole only buttons, but did so with religious seriousness. Hearthline relocated him seven times.

On the eighth, she stitched a row of spares inside the outer flap of her satchel.

They were gone by breakfast.

She counted that as a negotiated settlement.

Button returned some of them over the years, usually when a missing button would create maximum confusion. He returned every kind but one: small, pearl-white, and cut like an eye. Hearthline never remembered owning such a button. Button never admitted that he did, either.

The work suited her. Every town was a new machine, although she learned not to say that aloud. People disliked being called components even when they behaved exactly like jammed linkages.

Still, she believed most systems wanted to work.

Not magically. Not inevitably. Mills failed. Bridges rotted. Councils protected the wrong rules because the rules were older than the people being hurt by them. A mechanism could be designed badly, maintained carelessly, or made to serve a purpose it should never have served.

But if the parts could still be named, their relations could be examined. If damage had a path, repair might have one too.

That belief survived longer than her certainty did.

## III. The Winter Wheel

Her first serious failure happened at Drail's winter mill.

The lower wheel had begun striking once each rotation. Hearthline found a fractured guide, replaced it, and declared the mill safe through the first snow.

She had inspected the wheel.

She had not inspected the river wall carrying its load.

Three nights later, the wall shifted. The replacement guide held exactly as intended, transferring the force into the next weakest joint. The shaft leapt its cradle. Iron tore through the mill floor.

No one died. Tamsin pulled the miller's son clear and lost the full use of two fingers doing it. The village lost half its stored flour before the mechanism could be stopped.

Hearthline stood in the wreckage with her perfect new guide still bolted in place.

For several weeks, she spoke only when the repair required a number.

People mistook her silence for discipline. Inside, she had become afraid of confidence. Every finished repair looked like a place where another consequence might wait just beyond the frame.

Tamsin did not absolve her.

"You missed the wall," she said.

"I did."

"You answered a larger question than you inspected."

"I did."

"Will you do that again?"

Hearthline looked at the bandages around Tamsin's hand.

"Probably. Somewhere else. In a new shape I don't recognize."

"Better answer."

They rebuilt the mill.

Hearthline crawled every load path before setting the first tooth. She marked what she had inspected, what she had inferred, and what remained beyond reach. When the wheel turned again, the whole village waited for the strike.

It did not come.

No one cheered immediately. That made the later cheering honest.

Hearthline never recovered her earlier ease. She recovered something sturdier: the ability to work while knowing that care was not omniscience.

A hairline fragment from the broken guide remained in her satchel for years. Button never stole it.

## IV. Spark Weather

The first time Hearthline's pupils went pale, she was waist-deep in reservoir water arguing with twelve gremlins and a failing sluice gate.

Lightning had awakened an old current in the hill. Every metal fitting around the spillway began singing a different note. The gremlins, delirious with it, pulled pins faster than Hearthline could replace them.

She remembered the moment as pressure behind the eyes, then clarity.

Her irises remained green. The pupils within them softened from black into pearlescent white—not hard white circles, not blank eyes, but pale shapes with a faint green-cyan cast, bright enough to nearly disappear into the color around them. Fine seams of cyan traveled across the fittings of her staff. Every vibration separated into a readable strand.

Hearthline saw the gate as a field of relations: twelve hands, four loose pins, one trapped counterweight, two false solutions, and a path narrow enough to work.

She sent the current across the staff.

Every unsecured scrap of metal snapped harmlessly into the mud. The counterweight dropped. The gate opened in stages. Reservoir water passed beneath them without taking the village with it.

Then Hearthline fainted face-first into three inches of water.

The gremlins dragged her onto the bank, mostly because she was blocking the brightest current.

Children from the reservoir village called what they had seen **Spark Mode**. Hearthline objected that the name was imprecise.

The name survived the objection.

Spark Mode was never merely anger or power. It came when attention, danger, and a solvable structure aligned hard enough to pull every available part of her toward one problem. The glow around her varied. The staff could flare, hum, or remain nearly dark. The stable visible sign was in her eyes: green irises, pupils gone soft and pearlescent instead of black. They returned to black afterward.

Afterward also came trembling hands, headaches, hunger, and an overwhelming need to sleep somewhere no one was dismantling a sluice.

She learned not to invoke it merely because a task was difficult.

Some difficulties needed patience more than force.

Years later, under strong magnification, Tamsin thought she saw one ordinary black point remain at the center of each pale pupil. Hearthline saw it once and could never reproduce the angle. Neither of them wrote *mystery solved* on the page.

## V. The Little Lights

The first Spark emerged from an emberglass coil Hearthline built to watch six pressure needles at once.

She expected six colored indicators. Instead she got six bright little figures, each no taller than her hand, each carrying one fragment of the requested attention and absolutely none of the others.

One could find labels but would not open a case. One would inspect every part but touch nothing. One would happily tighten a bolt and just as happily tighten the wrong bolt unless its task was named precisely.

Hearthline was delighted.

Tamsin was not present to stop her from being delighted, which may explain why there were eventually more.

She gave them work-shaped names rather than pretending resemblance made them interchangeable: Valve Seeker, Bell Explorer, North-Rail Handler. A Spark that traveled received a marked route home. A Spark that returned unsuccessful still received its place by the fire.

Once, three Sparks came back carrying the same spoon.

None could explain why.

Hearthline kept the spoon.

One little Spark was kept out of every expedition. It lived beside the cold kettle at the home bench and was known, when Hearthline thought nobody listened, as Little Wick. Little Wick had one storybook job: keep one ember for the homecoming. If a traveling light missed the expected note, Wick gave one small pulse and waited.

It did not chase. It did not decide. It did not turn a silence into a conclusion.

It simply kept one ember waiting for the homecoming.

Once, long after every traveler had returned and been counted, Little Wick pulsed anyway.

Thulia was not yet there to object to the filing. Tamsin was. She wrote the time in the margin and drew a question mark so small it looked like smoke.

## VI. Thulia Corrects the Filing System

Thulia entered Hearthline's life by correcting her filing system.

At an abandoned customs lodge along the north road, Hearthline found numbered slips arranged across the floor. She assumed the wind had scattered them. She restored them by date.

A small owl watched from a beam.

The owl was mostly peat-charcoal and smoked umber, with a warm ash face and amber-brass eyes. Two short crest plumes sharpened when Hearthline placed slip nineteen before slip seventeen.

The owl descended, took nineteen in one foot, moved it three places to the right, and looked at Hearthline.

"The dates say—"

The owl tapped a faded route mark with her beak.

The slips were not chronological. They recorded crossings between the lodge, the bridge, and three vanished watch posts. Hearthline's arrangement had made the record tidier and the route false.

"Ah," Hearthline said.

The owl gave one slow blink.

For the next two days, they reconstructed the lodge records. Hearthline read what remained. The owl found which pieces belonged together. She never pushed two lineages into one pile merely because their paper had weathered to the same color.

When afternoon sun reached beneath the owl's facial disk, the two halves of a broken gorget woke from charcoal into antique gold. Small comma-shaped feathers at her shoulders answered in moss and peacock teal. A turn of her head carried restrained violet along two expressive crest plumes. Most of her stayed matte, and in shadow every Northlight color slept.

Hearthline stared through the lodge's cracked field lens until the owl clicked her beak.

"Sorry."

"Thu-li," said the owl.

"Hearthline," Hearthline replied.

That was how introductions worked from then on.

Thulia could leave whenever she wished. For nearly a month, she did. She reappeared at campsites, on signposts, and once inside Hearthline's satchel with Button crouched penitently beneath one wing.

Then a Spark failed to return from an old mine survey.

At dawn, Thulia arrived carrying the Spark's numbered home slip beneath her right foot and a strip of its torn route paper beneath her left. She turned toward the mine, then toward Hearthline, then flew.

The missing Spark was found beside a collapsed ventilation shaft, still pulsing its last little light into the dark.

Afterward, Hearthline built a dark rail above her traveling workbench. She made Thulia a narrow ember-copper band for her anatomical right leg, plain except for one identifying mark. The owl tolerated the band after inspecting it from every angle.

The Hearth Perch was wherever that rail could be securely mounted.

Thulia became Hearthline's companion, route-keeper, paper critic, and final authority on whether a shelf had been arranged offensively. She was not a Spark and did not become a tiny feathered commander simply because Hearthline trusted her.

She was not always present. She did not circle Hearthline like a decorative moon. Some evenings she hunted. Some journeys she vanished beyond the trees and returned at dawn with wet feathers and no explanation.

But when Hearthline worked late, there was often a soft weight on the rail beyond the brightest part of the fire.

The quiet became less empty.

One spring, Thulia returned from three absent nights with a paper fold naming a northern watch post as **Perch Zero**. No surviving map used that name. She tucked the fold under the rail and refused to discuss it in any language Hearthline understood.

## VII. The House of Open Doors

Not every town welcomed the arrangement.

At Copper Hall, the merchant council offered Hearthline a permanent workshop if her Sparks would inspect competing guilds' warehouses for "dangerous irregularities."

They offered good tools, clean rooms, and more money than she had earned in the previous two years.

Hearthline asked whether the other guilds had agreed to the inspection.

The council changed the subject.

She declined.

Three days later, a clockwork storehouse failed and released a colony of spark-fed gremlins into the market. The council blamed Hearthline's experiments. Her short-term bench lease disappeared. So did half her customers.

Thulia recovered a torn routing slip that placed council workers inside the storehouse before the failure. It proved a visit, not sabotage. Hearthline refused to make it carry more than that.

The full cause remained unresolved.

She repaired the market shutters anyway.

The worst hardship was not leaving Copper Hall. Hearthline had left towns before. It was recognizing how badly she wanted the workshop: the fixed bench, the dry roof, the possibility of becoming known somewhere through more than the damage she arrived to fix.

For several miles after the city gate, she was furious with herself for wanting it.

Thulia flew ahead and waited on the next milestone.

One shoulder comma caught copper in the evening light.

Hearthline sat beside her, divided an apple, and watched the city lamps appear behind them one by one.

The next morning, they continued.

## VIII. The Doorless Keep

The deep goblin keep beneath Mourningroot Wood had no doors.

It had arches, portcullises, murder holes, thresholds, hinges, locks, and several rooms that plainly required entrances. But every door had been removed.

Hearthline found the reason four levels down.

Something fifteen feet tall had been collecting them.

The creature called himself Brannoch. Others called him an ogre, a giant goblin, the Door King, or—at a safer distance—someone else's problem. He wore scavenged armor and carried a stone maul bound with brass straps. Behind him rose a throne made from two hundred stolen doors.

Hearthline looked very small from the far end of the hall.

That was accurate.

She lit a small fire beneath the broken gallery and divided the impossible among her Sparks. Three watched different parts of the hall while the others traced chains, pillars, vents, and exits. Thulia carried one route at a time between their separate perches. None was asked to carry the whole frightening keep in its tiny head.

Brannoch charged before they were ready.

The floor shook. Hearthline entered Spark Mode with her staff braced low. Her pale pupils caught the cyan lines waking along its shaft.

She smiled.

Not because Brannoch was harmless. Brannoch was extraordinarily harmful.

She smiled because the impossible size of him had finally become a specific engineering problem.

The fight crossed three halls and one stolen bakery door. Sparks pulled release pins. Thulia dropped a brass route tag through a gap in the gallery. Hearthline followed the falling tag, saw the cracked load pillar behind Brannoch's throne, and cast a line through its old support ring.

The throne collapsed around him without bringing down the keep.

Brannoch survived, trapped beneath enough doors to reconsider his professional direction.

The missing goblin families survived too. They had not been Brannoch's army. They had been his builders.

Hearthline stayed twelve days to restore the keep's entrances.

No two doors were returned to the correct room.

Everyone agreed this improved the place.

Brannoch wore a tiny silver bell around one wrist. It rang once after the throne fell, although its clapper was missing. Button, when asked, looked offended by the implication that bells counted as buttons.

## IX. The Rainbow Shell

In the lowest chamber, behind the only opening Brannoch had been afraid to wall shut, Hearthline found the Rainbow Shell.

It was not a shell in any ordinary biological sense.

Two prismatic spirals curved through one golden ring, each narrowing toward the other without touching. Faceted fields carried color around the circle—red through violet, then back by another route—while fine gold lines held the whole structure in tension.

It was beautiful in the dangerous way of devices whose makers are absent.

Hearthline did not touch it for three days.

She inspected its plinth, the chamber, every available inscription, and the scars on the walls. Thulia turned beneath Hearthline's lantern until her six Northlight fields passed through the shell's colors at different angles. Only then did hidden seams become visible.

The old device had not been built to create power. It appeared to resolve several incompatible forms of power into one directed release.

The empty center was the important part.

Hearthline removed it only after establishing that it was detachable, unclaimed by the keep's living residents, and unlikely to collapse the mountain. Even then, it spent months wrapped in wool and locked in Tamsin's stone cabinet.

Hearthline did not yet possess a portable lens fine enough to align it. The old bench glass showed that the Shell had layers and refused to say how many. Attempting a field mount anyway would have been confidence dressed as courage.

So she left it safe, carried a charcoal rubbing of its outer ring, and kept walking.

Thulia would sleep beside the closed cabinet, but never on it.

The bent brass strip Hearthline had held at Linehouse Nine matched the Shell's outer curve so closely that Tamsin stopped calling it coincidence. It fit no slot they could find.

They marked that as open too.

## X. The Road South

Years accumulated unevenly.

Tamsin retired from field work and began teaching left-handed apprentices to compensate for two stiff fingers. Button established an enormous cache somewhere beneath Cairnwater and occasionally mailed one piece back without postage. Hearthline's Sparks multiplied by jobs, not ambition. Thulia acquired a habit of placing unanswered letters beneath Hearthline's breakfast bowl.

Hearthline became known enough that rumors often arrived before she did.

Some called her a master repairer. Some called her an elf weaponwright. Some insisted she was the lightning-made daughter of Linehouse Nine. One tavern song gave her a silver dragon, a royal title, and an entirely fictional marriage to a prince she had actually rescued from a dumbwaiter.

She corrected the dangerous errors and let the funny ones travel.

There were hard seasons.

Thulia disappeared through one whole winter after a northern storm. Hearthline searched every recorded route, then stopped where the trace stopped. In spring, Thulia returned thin, carrying another numbered paper fold from a watch post no map admitted had ever existed.

Hearthline fed her before asking questions.

A bridge Hearthline repaired washed out seven years later. No fault in her work was established, but she went back and inspected the river anyway.

A Spark once came home bearing cold ash from no fire Hearthline could identify. When held beneath the old bench glass, it smelled briefly of rain.

Tamsin's workshop survived lightning, floodwater, Button, and three municipal improvement plans. It finally burned because someone left lamp oil beside a stove. No mystery improved the loss.

Hearthline rebuilt the public half and left the old foundation visible beneath it.

One stone was still warm.

She never recovered a trustworthy answer about Linehouse Nine. By then, she no longer needed an invented one.

She had Tamsin's bench, an owl on the Hearth Perch, a satchel full of distinct small lights, a ridiculous staff, more unfinished questions than when she began, and roads that recognized her boots.

Someone continued repairing the abandoned mechanisms at Linehouse Nine. The repairs were competent, recent, and never signed. Hearthline visited twice and found no one. On the third visit she left tea.

On the fourth, the cup had been washed.

## XI. One Minute Before Velis

The broken pocket watch reached her at a roadside inn north of Velis.

Its inner case bore the name **W. Vesper**. The works were fine, old, and stopped in a way the obvious damage did not explain. The courier who left it had received no payment address, only a note:

> *If the watch still refuses the hour, take it to Mira's clocks and locks in the South District.*

Hearthline examined it under her cracked field magnifier. Beneath the balance bridge waited a mark too fine for that tired glass to resolve.

It might have resembled the divided sun once scratched into Linehouse Nine's brass.

It might not.

She packed the watch beside her tool roll, counted every Spark's home slip, and waited while Thulia reordered two of them. The Rainbow Shell remained locked in Cairnwater. Tamsin kept one unopened letter addressed to Hearthline, written in a hand Tamsin recognized but would not name until Hearthline asked directly.

The road descended through wet hills toward Velis.

At `7:42 AM`, Hearthline stood beneath the South District awnings with rain stippling her scarf. Across the street, gold letters on a narrow shop window read:

**MIRA'S CLOCKS, LOCKS, AND SMALL REPAIRS**

The Vesper watch lay silent in her satchel.

Little Wick gave no warning. Thulia watched the door. Hearthline's pupils were ordinary black inside green.

For once, the next minute had not yet told her what it needed.

One minute remained before the established first day began.

Hearthline rested one hand over the watch in her satchel and waited for the minute to turn.

The rain kept the place for her.

## Things that did not ask to be solved yet

These are story openings, not hidden answers or operational claims:

- Button returned every stolen button except the pearl-white one that Hearthline never remembered owning.
- One Spark's home slip carries a faint handwritten correction Hearthline does not remember making.
- Somebody repairs Linehouse Nine and washes the cups left there.
- Tamsin's unopened letter is still unopened.
- The unreadable mark beneath the Vesper watch's balance bridge waits for a better lens.
- In certain light, one black point may remain inside Hearthline's pearlescent Spark Mode pupils.

The trace ends there because the next minute already has a story.
