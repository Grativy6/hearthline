# The Volunteer

> **Story status:** Fictional Hearthline lore
> **Continuity:** An undated cabin interlude after Velis, across several growing seasons
> **Operational effect:** None
> **Author and steward:** Christopher D. Pang

Hearthline found the plant while looking for a dropped button.

It had come up beside the cabin's lowest step, in a narrow pocket of dirt between a stone and the wood. Four little leaves leaned toward the afternoon sun. One still carried a grain of earth on its tip.

She brushed the earth away, then sat on the step to inspect the unexpected arrival.

There had been tomatoes with supper last summer. She remembered carrying the plates outside afterward, but not whether she had taken that path to the compost heap. Perhaps a seed had slipped from a plate. Perhaps it had arrived in the compost itself, or on a muddy boot, or by some route she had never seen.

Had it spent the winter there? Under the snow, beside all those passing feet?

Thulia watched from the porch rail.

“Do you know anything about this?” Hearthline asked.

The owl turned her head toward a sound in the trees.

“I thought not.”

Hearthline found the button farther down the path. When she came back, she stepped around the plant.

For a while, that was most of what she did.

She noticed when another pair of leaves appeared. During a dry spell, she gave the little pocket of soil a drink from the watering can. When the stem began leaning across the step, she found a stick and tied it loosely upright.

Then there were yellow flowers.

Then there were green tomatoes.

She checked them on her way out in the morning and usually forgot them until she came home. They managed the afternoons without her.

The first ripe one was small, warm, and imperfectly round. Hearthline ate it sitting on the steps, with a pinch of salt from the kitchen and the juice running over her thumb.

She looked at the plant.

“Well.”

A second tomato was nearly ready beneath the leaves.

“That's a very good trick.”

## Again

By the end of summer, the volunteer had contributed to sandwiches, two suppers, and several inspections that ended before the tomato reached the kitchen.

Hearthline saved a few seeds.

The following spring, she planted them in little pots on the windowsill. Most came up. When it was warm enough, she cleared a sunny patch near the cabin and gave them a bed of their own.

They grew there too.

She had tomatoes to share that summer. A neighbor brought back the empty bowl with some bean seeds inside. Someone else offered a pepper plant. Hearthline tucked basil along the warm edge of the bed, where she could reach it while gathering supper.

The garden smelled wonderful in the evening.

She ate outside more often. She learned which tomatoes ripened first and where the shade reached at four o'clock. The beans climbed their poles. The pepper plant took its time, then surprised her.

When unfamiliar seedlings appeared, she sometimes left them to see what they would become.

One made flowers. Bees found it.

Another turned out to be a cucumber she had entirely forgotten planting.

“You see?” she told Thulia. “Worth waiting.”

There was still room for the chair beside the steps. Still room to carry a bowl down the path without turning sideways. Still time, after watering, to sit and eat what she had picked.

So she saved more seeds.

## More

The next windowsill filled quickly.

Hearthline added a shelf, then borrowed the sunny end of the table. She planted enough for another bed and a few extra pots in case something failed. Very little failed.

At planting time, she stood over the remaining seedlings with a trowel in her hand.

There was a little space beside the onions.

There was some behind the beans.

The path was rather wide, really.

Each new place held one small plant. For weeks, the arrangement seemed perfectly reasonable.

Then the small plants grew.

Tomato leaves reached across the onions. A cucumber caught the bean trellis and climbed into it. The flower she had welcomed for the bees scattered seed along the path, and the path became a border, and the border became something she had to step over.

Other things arrived too: grass in the beds, bindweed winding through the stems, tall strangers that grew vigorously and gave her no reason to trust them except that they were growing.

She hesitated over the unfamiliar ones. She had nearly overlooked the first tomato. What if this one was useful? What if that one flowered?

She would wait another day.

Meanwhile, the plants she had chosen needed help.

She put up more stakes. She tied another row of strings. She moved the chair to make room for a tub, then moved the tub so she could reach a thirsty plant behind it.

There were tomatoes everywhere.

This remained exciting for quite a long time.

She filled bowls, roasted trays, and left little gifts on neighbors' doorsteps. One afternoon she carried home four empty bowls and a jar of sauce made from tomatoes she had given away. She laughed all the way up the path.

A week later, she could not find the path.

She could find where it had been. Two flat stones showed through the leaves, and she remembered a third near the watering can. Reaching the can required stepping across the cucumber, lifting a tomato branch, and bracing one foot somewhere she hoped nothing important was growing.

She did it carefully.

The sleeve of her coat caught a stake. Three tomatoes fell. While she reached for them, the watering can tipped against her boot.

Hearthline stood very still in the green tangle, holding a branch above her head.

From the porch rail, Thulia watched her.

“Don't,” Hearthline said.

Thulia said nothing.

That was somehow worse.

## A place to sit

That evening, Hearthline took her supper outside and discovered that the chair was still behind the shed.

She had moved it there nine days earlier.

She ate on the top step, where the leaves had not yet reached, and looked down into the garden. Beneath the tallest tomato, the basil had become pale and thin. The onions were mostly hidden. Bindweed had pulled one flower stem sideways until its head rested in the dirt.

There were ripe tomatoes she could see and could not comfortably reach.

She had worked in the garden every evening that week. She could not remember the last evening she had spent simply sitting in it.

After supper, she brought the chair back.

To set it down, she had to move two pots and cut a trailing stem. She carried one pot to the porch and the other to the sunny side of the shed. Neither plant appeared offended by the change of address.

The next morning, she began with the path.

Some plants were small enough to lift and offer to neighbors. Some could be tied back. She worked the bindweed loose and dug after its roots. Other plants came out altogether.

She disliked pulling the first healthy tomato.

She had raised it from a seed on her windowsill. It had roots, flowers, and several green fruits. She stood with her hand around the stem for a long time.

Then she looked at the basil beneath it.

She gathered the green tomatoes before she cut it back. The stems went to the compost heap. Sunlight reached the ground where it had stood.

There were mistakes in the tending too. A transplanted flower wilted. She cut one branch she meant to keep. Roots remained where she thought she had removed them, and a week later she was kneeling in the same place again.

But the path widened.

The basil put out new leaves.

One evening, she picked an onion without first untangling anything from it. She held it up, delighted by an accomplishment she would once have considered beneath notice.

By late summer, the garden held fewer tomato plants. She could reach all of them.

There were peppers warming against the wall, beans on their own poles, and flowers where she could see them from the chair. Beyond the beds, she left a loose patch for bees and the sorts of arrivals she did not need to name immediately.

She still watered. She still made room for something promising now and then. When a new plant went in, she pictured the size it might become and where her feet would have to go beside it.

And when supper was ready, she often put the watering can down.

## The little tomato

The next spring, a green sprout appeared beside the lowest step.

Hearthline found it while sweeping.

She set the broom against the rail and crouched beside it. The tiny leaves were familiar now, although that told her nothing certain about how this particular seed had arrived.

Last summer's supper, perhaps. Something spilled from a bowl. One of the tomatoes she had dropped while wrestling with the watering can.

“Back again?” she asked.

The sprout offered no account of itself.

She looked at the open path, the bed waiting for onions, and the chair with its feet planted firmly where she wanted them.

There was a small, sunny pocket beside the step. A grown tomato could lean away from the walking space if she gave it a stake on the other side.

“Have we got room for you?”

She considered it properly.

Then she went to fetch a stick.

Months later, she sat in the chair with a plate on her knees. There were sliced tomatoes, bread, a little cheese, and green leaves she had reached without difficulty. The garden stirred around her in the evening air. Thulia settled on the rail above the steps.

Hearthline chose the smallest tomato and turned it once between her fingers.

“Still my favorite,” she said.

She never did find out where the first seed had come from.

When she carried the plate inside, a seed clung to its rim. She saw it at the door and paused, smiling.

This one went into a small pot.
