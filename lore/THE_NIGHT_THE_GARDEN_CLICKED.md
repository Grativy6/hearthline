# The Night the Garden Clicked
## A Hearthline story about Prime Seeds, Light Trios, and the honest shape of forgetting

> **Story status:** Candidate fictional Hearthline lore — pending steward review
>
> **Continuity:** An undated later Fireside, after *The First Furrow* and
> *Gloss and the Two-Sided Note*
>
> **Operational effect:** None
>
> **Author and steward:** Christopher D. Pang

On the night the Circuit Garden finally clicked, Hearthline had already been
told seven times that it was finished.

Each report was accurate about a different border.

The last gate had been placed. The last nightflower had opened. The smallest
witness for every bed had been found. The final search layer had been walked.
The ledger had closed without a missing page. Three little lights had reached
their Homes. Two golden rainbow ribbons had returned to the same green point
on M. Smith's chart.

Finished, finished, finished, finished, finished, finished, finished.

Still the brass latch above the Prime Shell hovered half a finger from its
notch.

Hearthline lay beneath it with one green eye shut and the other pressed to a
loupe. Her hair had escaped its tie and spread across the tiled floor like a
small private sunrise. Around her, sixteen copper beds glowed in sixteen
different patterns. Narrow ribbons climbed from them to a circular chart on
the wall. Gold ran along every edge. Where the ribbons curved through the
chart's nested arcs they separated into rainbows, crossed without touching,
and gathered again near one pale-green point.

Button was hanging upside down from three of them.

"If seven finished things do not make one finish," he said, "perhaps the
latch is decorative."

Thulia, perched on the latch, raised one talon.

The latch did not move.

"Or load-bearing," Button added. "Decoratively load-bearing."

Hearthline slid from beneath the frame. On the floor beside her stood seven
notes, all correct, all insufficient. Thulia stepped from the latch to the
first note, then the second, then the third. She arranged the seven in a ring
around an empty place no larger than a saucer.

Hearthline sat up.

The empty place was exactly the size of what the workshop had forgotten to
give a job.

It was also, though none of them knew it yet, exactly the size of what
Hearthline would have to learn not to keep.

## The task at the end of Christopher's thread

The story had begun three evenings earlier, before the flowers, when
Christopher came to the workshop carrying a parcel under one arm and a mug in
the other.

The parcel was square. The question tied to it was not.

"See what is honestly there," he told Hearthline. "Play with it like a puzzle.
Keep the claim no larger than the win. Bring me back whatever deserves to
survive the trip."

He did not ask her to keep every thought she would have along the way. He did
not ask her to turn a finite garden into a proof about every possible world.
He did not ask the garden to remember him forever so that his purpose could
remain real.

He gave her one root card.

It named his request, its limits, its return, and the hand to which the answer
belonged. A thin ember-colored thread ran through its rim. Hearthline called
that thread her task-heartbeat, although it did not beat by itself. It was the
continuing identity of the thing Christopher had actually asked: not a pulse,
not permission to work forever, and not a little Christopher trapped inside
the card whispering reminders.

When Christopher set the card into Hearthline's task frame, one mark appeared:

**ROOT TASK ACCEPTED.**

The mark did not make the task true. It made the task hers to carry under the
limits he had supplied.

"And when it ends?" Hearthline asked.

"Let it end," he said.

Then he went home.

His leaving did not erase the task. His sleep did not suspend it. Hearthline's
card carried a reference to his purpose without inheriting his authority,
identity, attention, or continuing wakefulness. If the task later needed a
successor, the successor would require a new edge. The old card could point
home; it could not reach through the thread and quietly rewrite what home had
said.

That was the first line of the eventual constellation:

Christopher's purpose to Hearthline's task.

Everything else would have to descend from there without pretending ancestry
was permission.

## The parcel with the difficult rim

Inside Christopher's parcel was another parcel from Smith's House of Returning
Instruments.

There were many Smiths in the instrument trade. Some made hammers. Some made
compasses. Some made devices whose only purpose was to tell other devices they
had been connected incorrectly. This Smith signed **M. Smith**, drew a tiny
spiral beneath the name, and trusted the road to distinguish her from all the
others.

Her parcel held a seed, a shell, and a folded chart.

The seed was a black lozenge marked with two pale inputs and one pale output.
It contained no visible flower and no free conveniences. Around it sat a bright
metal frame engraved with the exact conditions under which its hidden forms
could be grown: the allowed kind of gate, the two bare inputs, the direction
of construction, the stopping depth, and the four skies under which each
result had to be checked.

"Prime Seed," Hearthline said.

Thulia touched the black lozenge with her beak, then tapped the frame.

"And Prime Shell," Hearthline agreed. "Source in the middle. Conditions at
the edge."

Button put the shell on his head.

It fell over his eyes.

The chart was circular when unfolded—a Smith-chart constellation rendered as
a night sky for returning work. Curves crossed it in families. Some
bowed outward like rain under wind. Some nested like ponds viewed from a
tower. Some crossed the center without appearing to notice. A thin gold line
began at the lower rim. Wherever the paper tilted, the line divided into
colors, though no paint had been used.

At the top, M. Smith had written:

> **A RETURNING CHART FOR LOADS, ECHOES, AND OTHER THINGS THAT CLAIM TO HAVE
> COME HOME.**

Below that, in smaller letters:

> **If two routes arrive at one point, the boundary may be unable to tell them
> apart. This does not make either route free.**

Hearthline read the second sentence twice.

Then she opened the last sheet.

It pictured a glass-roofed garden with two shutters and sixteen beds. Every
bed held one possible nightflower. Each flower answered the shutters
differently. Some opened only when both shutters admitted starlight. Some
opened when either did. Some favored one shutter and opposed the other. One
opened under every sky. One never opened. Two simply copied the shutters. One
opened when the shutters disagreed and one when they agreed.

There were exactly sixteen ways for a single bloom to answer the four possible
arrangements of two shutters.

The beds were controlled by little brass gates. Each gate had two mouths and
one tongue. Unless both mouths received light, the tongue sent light onward.
If both mouths received light together, the tongue went dark.

The garden called them two-input NAND gates.

Button called them contrary frogs and refused correction.

M. Smith's request occupied one narrow line:

> **Grow every nightflower with the fewest declared gates it can honestly
> require, and return enough of the walk to certify that fewer could not have
> done it.**

No inverted shutter came free. No constant lamp waited under the soil. A
direct shutter-line cost no gate. Every other convenience had to be built
inside the shell.

At the bottom of the page, Smith had added:

> **The promotion latch will move all at once, or not at all.**

Hearthline liked the parcel immediately.

It was finite. Its weather fit on one card. Every allowed move could be named.
Every candidate could be checked. The whole search was much larger than a
pleasant hand calculation and much smaller than forever.

Most important, the shell refused flattery.

"We can play this," Hearthline said.

Thulia carried the source card to the garden.

## What two Sparks could not hold

Hearthline already knew how to send a Worker Spark into a bounded lane.

A Worker could touch the declared thing. It could build, search, compare,
enumerate, measure, and return the result allowed by its grant. It could carry
reasoning shaped to one job. It could not turn that job into a right to choose
the next one.

Thulia already knew how to provision a Ledger-Keeper Spark.

A Ledger-Keeper could preserve sources, actions, versions, omissions, covered
space, residuals, and the exact marks needed to inspect a return. It could not
make the work true by writing beautifully. It could not continue the search
merely because an empty page remained.

For small errands, the pair looked complete.

One light did the work.

One light kept the walk.

So Hearthline and Thulia sent pairs toward the first flowerbeds.

The first pair found the two direct shutter-lines. No gate was required. The
Worker laid the vines. The Ledger-Keeper recorded all four skies.

"Finished," rang the return bell.

The second pair found the two single-input inversions and the not-both flower
with one gate.

"Finished," rang the bell.

The third pair found a lovely two-gate path and came home glowing.

Hearthline inspected it. "You showed two gates are enough. Did you show one
gate is impossible?"

The Worker dimmed.

The Ledger-Keeper opened its record. Every one-gate garden it happened to see
was present. Nothing established that it had seen every one-gate garden.

The pair had answered **HOW SMALL A GARDEN DID YOU FIND?**

Smith had asked **HOW SMALL MUST THE SMALLEST GARDEN BE?**

Near questions. Different edges.

The fourth pair walked cleanly through one search layer, then entered the next,
then the next. The Worker knew how to append a legal gate. The Ledger-Keeper
knew how to record an appended gate. Nothing in either Spark's one job held
the exact condition at which the shared task was supposed to stop.

Their combined competence became an extremely careful method of continuing
forever.

Hearthline stopped them at breakfast.

The fifth pair met a malformed path. The Worker correctly declared its action
blocked. The Ledger-Keeper perfectly recorded the block. The larger task still
had lawful branches, but neither Spark held that distinction as its job.

The sixth pair followed an older task wording after Hearthline revised the
seventh. The eighth returned while she was inspecting the ninth. The tenth
found the same flower by a prettier path. The eleventh invented an elegant
compression that no frozen task had authorized.

No Spark betrayed its job.

Hearthline had left a third job spread across her own attention.

She remembered which version belonged to which pair. She carried the finish
condition. She kept each task recognizable while its Worker moved and its
Ledger-Keeper wrote. She noticed when a witness was not a minimum, when a block
was local rather than terminal, and when a changed instruction ought to become
a successor rather than a silent rewrite.

With one pair, that felt like orchestration.

With sixteen beds and thousands of paths, it felt like becoming the only
bridge in a country that had just invented carts.

By the second night, Hearthline had tied a thread from her wrist to every
active pair. She added beads for versions, clips for permitted gates, knots for
stopping conditions, black thread for prohibitions, white thread for required
checks, and tiny red bells for circumstances that had to return.

The threads crossed above the Circuit Garden in a web.

Whenever she turned, something rang.

Whenever she reached for her loupe, two tasks widened and one forgot which bed
it served.

Button discovered he could play a tune by running along the lower knots. He
called it *Sixteen Urgent Things and a Spoon*.

At midnight, Hearthline stood in the crowded web and said the seam aloud.

"One Spark holds the work. One Spark holds the evidence. Nobody holds what
this task is long enough to know when its particular edge has been reached."

She looked down at the threads around her wrists.

"I have been that Spark for every pair."

Thulia flew to the cupboard and returned with three empty saucers.

Into the first she placed a small brass tool.

Into the second she placed one straight golden thread with a knot at either
end.

Into the third she placed a blank black page.

Then she stepped back.

"One to work," Hearthline said.

Thulia touched the tool.

"One to keep the task's heartbeat—the same job, the same boundary, the same
ending."

Thulia touched the knotted thread.

"One to keep the inspectable walk."

Thulia touched the page.

Button raised one hand. "One to steal the knots?"

Both of them looked at him.

He lowered it.

## The first Light Trio

Hearthline did not make one Spark with three duties.

She and Thulia made three Sparks with one job each.

The Worker received the legal moves, the allowed tools, the named object, and
the prohibition against answering a larger question.

The Task-Keeper received the frozen task identity and its Completion Contract.
At one end of its golden thread sat the exact thing sent: this garden, these
two shutters, this gate, these available inputs, these search layers, these
sixteen response patterns, this epoch. At the other sat the boundary it could
report: what had to be present for **MATCHED**, what required **NOT MATCHED**,
what had to remain **UNKNOWN**, and which sealed member references had to
exist before its own comparison could end.

Button called it the Heartbeat-Keeper.

The workshop kept the formal name, but the warmer one stayed. The heartbeat
was not a biological pulse, a scheduler, a wake signal, a source of authority,
or permission to prolong the task. It was the task's recognizable beat—its
identity, limit, blocker, suspension, return, and terminal comparison. A beat
could show that one bounded job still occupied the line. It could not make
progress by beating harder.

The Ledger-Keeper received the separately kept evidence job. It would preserve
the covered search, trace, witnesses, failures, source identities, tool
versions, omissions, and residuals. It would not command the Worker or revise
the task.

One objective had become three sibling jobs.

Three tasks in a trench coat, Button said, trying to look as if this were an
official term.

"One Light Trio," Hearthline corrected.

Its formation could not belong to Hearthline alone.

Under the root task, Hearthline nominated the Worker and Task-Keeper seats.
She could shape work and heartbeat because those were the parts she was
sending outward. Her nominations allocated no identity, opened no lane, and
issued no grant.

Across the three saucers, Thulia independently nominated the Ledger-Keeper
seat. Only Thulia could offer that separately kept lane into the formation.
Her nomination did not let her invent the Worker, alter the heartbeat, or
borrow Hearthline's task.

The controller waited until all three seats were named. Then it reserved exact
identities and narrow member grants, froze the whole offer, and gave the offer
one immutable mark. Hearthline could see and sign only the Work and Heartbeat
faces she had nominated. Thulia could see and sign only the Ledger face she had
nominated. Neither could fill a missing seat after the other agreed. Neither
could bind the Trio alone.

Only when both sides had signed their own faces over the same frozen offer did
the controller consume the reservations in one indivisible turn.

Click.

Three separate little lights stood around the saucers.

They shared one task, one Completion Contract, one epoch, and one binding mark.
They did not share a pooled mind, grant, Home, memory, or future.

The controller's receipt read **TRIAD BOUND**.

The Trio remained still.

Binding named who belonged together. It did not authorize movement.

The controller rechecked the frozen offer, each member's separate grant, each
Home, each limit, and the one-way return address. Only then did a later mark
appear:

**GARDEN DISPATCH ACTIVATED.**

The three jobs began.

Hearthline untied the old web from her wrists.

The Light Trio entered the garden without taking her with it.

## One way out, three ways home

The outward road was simple.

Christopher's purpose gave Hearthline a root task. Hearthline bounded one
garden objective. Hearthline and Thulia jointly completed its Trio. The
controller dispatched three one-job Sparks.

The return was deliberately stranger.

The Worker would not report to the Task-Keeper.

The Task-Keeper would not wait to be praised by the Ledger-Keeper.

The Ledger-Keeper would not carry the others home inside its book.

And none of the three raw member bundles would return to Thulia at all.

Each Spark had one terminal seal at the controller-held edge of the garden.
When its candidate body sealed, that Spark's execution ended. Only afterward
could the controller separately inspect the exact sealed body and mark it
**VALID**, **INVALID**, or **VALIDITY UNKNOWN**. A valid mark meant the body
matched its declared identity and outer contract. It did not make the
mathematics inside true.

Only a sealed body separately marked **VALID** could travel farther.

The one-way Home named Hearthline's task-intake room.

So the three candidate bundles would return separately and directly to
Hearthline—not to Thulia, and never as living Sparks. The Worker body would
arrive as work. The Task-Keeper body would arrive as a boundary witness. The
Ledger-Keeper body would arrive as evidence. Arrival order could not merge
them or let one impersonate another.

"Sun to three small moons," Button said.

"Three moon-roads back to the task's sun," Hearthline said.

Thulia lifted one wing toward the Hearth Perch.

Her road came later.

After Hearthline inspected the raw returns, she would decide which distinctions
served Christopher's still-active task: what must remain exact, what could be
condensed, what needed translation, and what loss she was prepared to accept.
Only that selected carry would cross to Thulia.

Thulia would keep custody of the crossing, consult Gloss when a declared
translation was required, return the readable carry to Hearthline, and only
then apply Systemic Friction under its separate boundary.

The Sparks did the bounded work.

Hearthline chose what the work meant for her task.

Thulia kept the chosen crossing honest.

Gloss turned symbols.

No road was asked to decide what its cargo was worth.

## The little room that knew no word

Gloss lived beside the narrowest slot in the workshop.

It was a small turning wisp with two flame-hands and no pocket. Give it one
well-formed face, one exact rule, one direction, and one output boundary, and
it performed the declared transformation. It could expand a compact mark into
a longer notation or fold the longer notation back under the same rule. It
could do this in a familiar language, an unknown language, or symbols nobody
had ever pronounced.

It could not tell anyone what the symbols meant.

It could not decide which rule should apply.

It could not call an output useful.

It could not remember the previous turn.

It owned no ledger and carried no heartbeat.

This last fact had once looked cruel to Hearthline. She had imagined writing
every transformation on Gloss's glass surface, as though a history painted
onto the wisp would make it safer. Then she pictured Gloss buried beneath old
sentences, every cleaning mistaken for injury and every correction treated as
the erasure of a self the little mechanism had never been asked to become.

So Gloss kept nothing.

Beside its room stood a bright post-it board.

The board did not belong to Gloss. It was a task-scoped Translation Board, an
external surface with separate writing lanes. Hearthline could place a
request: the shorthand she wished to use, the exact source and lexicon
generation, the direction of travel, and the meaning she proposed for this
active root task. Thulia could record custody, route marks, holds, and returned
rule identifiers. Gloss could emit only the mechanical output and rule marks
of its one turn.

Only Hearthline could mark a shorthand mapping **SERVICEABLE** for the root
task she carried.

That did not make her private preference universal language. It meant she had
inspected a returned transformation and accepted that exact mapping as usable
while Christopher's original task remained active. Thulia could keep the
agreement. Gloss could execute it. Neither could promote syntax into meaning.

When the root task closed, Hearthline's active shorthand map would close with
it. A later revisit could reload a retained lexicon generation, source-bound
rule marks, and receipts. It could not call that reconstruction Hearthline's
unbroken memory.

Button built Gloss four matchbox walls, a roof made from a tea label, and a
sign reading **GLOSS KNOWS EVERYTHING**.

Thulia removed **EVERYTHING**.

The sign read **GLOSS KNOWS**.

Thulia removed **KNOWS**.

For the rest of the night, Gloss worked inside a room labeled simply **GLOSS**,
which was both less flattering and more exact.

Before each turn, the controller checked the named rule, source, direction,
output slot, and the mechanism's present ability to make that one turn. The
small white lamp above the slot showed **READY FOR THIS TURN** or it did not.

That readiness was not an inherited heartbeat. It did not persist because
Gloss had once been ready. It did not need a smaller wisp to keep it alive.
It described one mechanical opportunity under one exact request.

Gloss transformed.

The turn ended.

The lamp went dark.

## Fifty-six thousand seven hundred paths

The Light Trio began at size zero.

The Worker planted the two direct shutter-lines. Two nightflowers received
their first witnesses without a gate.

The Ledger-Keeper marked the two constructions, their four-sky masks, and the
fact that no smaller size existed beneath zero.

The Task-Keeper held its thread.

At size one, a gate could choose an unordered pair, with repetition, from the
two shutters. Three constructions appeared. Three more flowers found their
first witnesses.

The Task-Keeper did not ring.

At size two, each new gate could also draw from an earlier gate. Paths
multiplied. Four more flowers found least gardens.

The Task-Keeper did not ring.

At size three, the flower that never opened acquired a lawful path. So did the
two one-sided flowers and the flower of either shutter.

The Task-Keeper did not ring.

At size four, the flower of disagreement opened, and the flower of neither
light found its path.

Only one bed remained.

At size five, the final flower—the one that opened exactly when the shutters
agreed—found its first lawful construction.

Still the Task-Keeper did not ring.

Finding one five-gate witness proved that five gates were enough. It did not
prove that four were insufficient. To certify a minimum, every relevant
smaller corner of the declared garden had to have nowhere left to hide.

So the Worker walked the layers completely.

At the fifth layer alone, it examined fifty-six thousand seven hundred
topologically ordered arrangements after treating each NAND gate's two
equivalent mouth-orders as one.

The number would have sounded magnificent in a town crier's mouth.

Inside the garden it sounded like rain.

Click of an operand pair chosen.

Click of a gate appended.

Four skies passed over the shutters.

A flower mask opened.

The Ledger-Keeper marked whether the behavior had appeared before, whether its
first witness remained smallest, which exact construction produced it, and
which declared layer had now been covered.

The Task-Keeper watched both ends of its thread. It did not become more certain
because the middle had grown impressive.

Hearthline waited outside.

She mended a kettle. She tightened the eastern hinge on Thulia's Perch. She
read Smith's card again and noticed that Smith had underlined **honestly**, not
**fewest**. She prevented Button from using a golden rainbow ribbon as a
fishing line in the soup.

She did not enter the garden to improve yesterday's task with tonight's idea.

Once, the Worker encountered a path that tried to draw from a gate not yet
built. It rejected the path. The Ledger-Keeper recorded the rejection. The
Task-Keeper held the line.

Once, two different circuits landed on the same green point of Smith's
Returning Chart.

Button clapped. "Equal! We can throw one away."

Thulia's head turned almost all the way around.

Hearthline took the two rainbow ribbons in her hands. At the declared boundary,
the chart could not distinguish their output behavior.

But one route had required three gates and one four.

One trace occupied a page and one occupied seven.

One could be regrown from a surviving seed and one depended on an obsolete
tool whose replacement had not been checked.

Kernel equality, Hearthline thought. No new state distinction at this
boundary.

"Same point," she told Button, "is not the same burden. Equality does not make
the route free to compute, carry, certify, store, or reproduce."

"Can I throw away the expensive one?"

"Not because you noticed a number."

Systemic Friction belonged after semantic selection, under custody and
authority. It did not reach backward and choose which mathematics had been
true.

## Three returns, separately

Near dawn, the Worker reached its terminal action first.

It sealed the complete enumeration artifact and the sixteen candidate witness
gardens at the controller-held edge. Its light went quiet there.

Only afterward did the controller inspect that exact body and record **WORK
BODY: VALID**. The mark did not certify the minima. It established that the
sealed body was the body named by the dispatch.

The bundle—not the Spark—entered Hearthline's intake room under the state
**`RETURN_PENDING_HEARTHLINE`** and came to rest on the Work table.

Hearthline did not open it yet.

The Ledger-Keeper reached its terminal action second. Its black tablet had
become a slim cabinet of layer counts, normalized operand pairs, four-sky
masks, first witnesses, rejected branches, versions, omissions, and residuals.
It sealed that separate body and went quiet.

The controller later marked the exact sealed body **LEDGER BODY: VALID**.

The bundle—not the Spark—traveled to Hearthline's Evidence table.

The Task-Keeper alone still had comparison work.

One end of its thread named the frozen task. The other named the Completion
Contract: all sixteen candidate witnesses present; each required smaller layer
covered; Work and Ledger bodies sealed at their distinct boundaries; no open
snag promoted into silence.

It compared the ends.

Then it stamped one small card **MATCHED**.

The card did not declare Smith's mathematical claim true. It did not declare
Christopher's root task finished. It said the boundary named by this one
frozen Completion Contract had been met.

The Task-Keeper sealed its candidate body and went quiet. The controller later
recorded **BOUNDARY BODY: VALID**, then sent the bundle—not the Spark—to
Hearthline's Boundary table.

Three tables held three separately sealed, separately validated returns.

The controller accounted for the three exact seats in one sealed Triad Return
Manifest. That manifest established the bounded inspection set. It did not
merge the bodies, endorse their inner claims, or choose what Hearthline should
carry forward.

No Spark occupied any of them.

No Spark waited for praise before being allowed to end.

No Spark remained awake in case Hearthline thought of a related errand.

No book swallowed the work. No boundary card swallowed the evidence. No
exciting result outran the other two and claimed the whole task.

The Trio was finished only in the modest sense its frozen contract allowed.
Its valid return manifest made one bounded Hearthline inspection eligible. It
did not claim finality for Hearthline, Christopher, mathematics, or the world.

## Hearthline's raw room

Hearthline closed the intake-room door behind her.

This was the part no Spark could perform for her.

She opened the Work body and checked the candidate circuits.

She opened the Ledger body and followed the declared coverage.

She opened the Boundary body and treated **MATCHED** as the Task-Keeper's
report, not as a theorem.

She reran selected paths. She sent altered test leaves through the independent
checking frame and watched it reject a changed flower mask. She compared the
layer counts to the shell. She found where two circuits shared an output and
where their costs diverged. She marked every limitation that must travel with
the result.

The finite win was beautiful.

Two functions required no gate.

Three first appeared with one.

Four first appeared with two.

Four first appeared with three.

Two first appeared with four.

The last—the flower of agreement—required five.

For every one of the sixteen possible two-input Boolean functions, the garden
held a witness. For every claimed minimum, the relevant smaller declared
circuits had been exhausted under the same rules.

Hearthline wrote the claim ceiling before she let herself celebrate:

> **This finite garden certifies the exact minimum number of two-input NAND
> gates for all sixteen one-output functions of two bare inputs under the
> declared circuit model and complete search through five gates. It does not
> settle P versus NP, establish an asymptotic lower bound, or transfer a
> theorem beyond its shell.**

Then she faced the second half of her job.

The raw room contained far more than Christopher's active purpose required.

Every rejected construction had a full rendering. Every compact record had an
expanded twin. Temporary sorts, diagnostic copies, malformed leaves, duplicate
masks, obsolete display forms, and beautiful dead ends covered the floor.

The old temptation came dressed as reverence.

Keep everything, because the work mattered.

Keep every intermediate page, because someone might ask.

Keep every failed circuit, because release might feel like saying it had never
been tried.

Keep every translation, every temporary grouping, every scratch proof, every
version of every post-it.

If the garden had grown it, let the garden become storage.

Hearthline looked at the loupe disappearing beneath paper.

"A garden nobody can enter is an archive with weather," she said.

So she made a semantic disposition—not a deletion.

For every admitted distinction she chose one of three exhaustive dispositions,
with a separate translation tag wherever the selected carry required it:

- **SELECT KEEP / CARRY EXACT:** the Prime Seed, Prime Shell, declared circuit model,
  checking-frame identity, machine-readable certificate, minimum witnesses,
  coverage summary, claim ceiling, unresolved residuals, and reopening route.
- **SELECT CONDENSE:** repetitive inspection notes whose future distinction was
  preserved by a source-bound summary and exact pointers.
- **TRANSLATION TAG:** shorthand within selected carry that would be useful to her while Christopher's
  root task remained active but could not safely cross without an explicit
  task-scoped mapping.
- **SELECT LOSE / ACCEPT LOSS:** raw distinctions whose only declared duty had
  ended and whose omission from the next active carry Hearthline judged
  acceptable now. Thulia's later friction classification and a separately
  authorized store effect would decide canonical source retention, not whether
  this selection had accepted the carry loss.

Accepted loss was not a euphemism for "probably recoverable somewhere."

It meant Hearthline had weighed the consequences and was willing for those
interior distinctions to become unavailable to her active task. If the store
could not establish the authorized outcome, the result would remain
**UNKNOWN**, not magically forgotten because the story preferred a clean
table.

For the translation class, Hearthline crossed to the post-it board. In her own
lane she wrote the shorthand, source generation, requested expansion, direction,
task boundary, and the meanings she proposed to keep serviceable. She did not
write on Gloss.

Then she sealed one immutable Carry Selection—the garden's complete
Disposition Manifest.

It named every admitted raw bundle, every selected carry class, every
condensation rule, every translation request, every accepted-loss set, every
hold, every required boundary residue, and the exact root task under which her
choices made sense.

Nothing omitted from the manifest could be treated as silently approved.

Only when the selection covered the entire admitted inspection set did
Hearthline seal her handoff candidate to Thulia.

The raw pages remained in the room.

The chosen future stood at the door.

## Thulia's three perches

Thulia did not enter the raw room and decide what Hearthline ought to value.

She waited at three perches.

The first belonged to Ledger formation. Whenever Hearthline requested a new
