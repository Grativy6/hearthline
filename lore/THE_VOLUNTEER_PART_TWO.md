# The Volunteer, Part II: The Garden Beneath

> **Story status:** Fictional Hearthline lore
> **Continuity:** Later seasons at the cabin, following [The Volunteer](THE_VOLUNTEER.md); Echo's visit is an imagined later meeting, not an advance of an active campaign
> **Operational effect:** None
> **Author and steward:** Christopher D. Pang

The woman who had given Hearthline the bean seeds came back for her bowl.

This was the third bowl. Neither of them had a reliable account of the first two, although both suspected tomatoes were involved.

She found Hearthline sitting beside the garden, holding a sketch of a trellis against the afternoon sky.

“More beans?”

“Better bean arrangements,” Hearthline said. “I've learned about more.”

The woman looked down the open path. Nothing caught her sleeve. The chair stood where a chair could reasonably expect to stand.

“So I see.”

Hearthline fetched the bowl. The woman set it on the step to study the drawing.

“Have you ever let corn do some of that?”

## Good company

Hearthline visited her neighbor's garden the following week.

Corn rose in a sunny block. Beans climbed the stalks, and squash spread broad leaves over the ground between the plantings. There was plenty growing, but it occupied the space differently from Hearthline's former tangle of tomatoes.

The neighbor called the planting the Three Sisters.

“Did you work this out?” Hearthline asked.

“Oh, no. I was taught. There's a long history here before either of our gardens.”

She showed Hearthline where she had started the corn first, giving it time to stand before the beans needed something to climb. She lifted a squash leaf so they could see the shaded soil beneath it.

Hearthline crouched beside her.

“And the beans feed the others?”

“People say that rather quickly.”

The neighbor explained about the bacteria in the roots, and how much of the nitrogen went into the bean plant itself. Some could eventually return through decay. The beans they carried into the kitchen took their share with them.

“You still have to look after the soil.”

Hearthline had already begun drawing a little arrow from the beans to the corn. She stopped, then wrote a few words beside it.

The neighbor leaned over.

“What does that say?”

“Ask when.”

They ate lunch in the shade. Afterward, the neighbor showed her where to reach the crops at harvest without stepping on the squash.

Hearthline paid particular attention to that part.

The next spring, she tried a modest planting of her own. It took some choosing: corn sturdy enough for the beans she wanted, a squash that would fit, a patch with enough sun. She kept the frequently picked vegetables beside the main path and let the crops intended to dry have their own place farther back.

One bean missed its stalk and found the fence. Hearthline guided another before it followed. A squash leaf reached into the walkway; she adjusted the vine while it was still easy to move.

The garden continued to require a gardener.

But several of her new stakes remained in the shed.

## What went home in the bowl

There were good seasons after that.

There were also small onions, reluctant peppers, and one bed that seemed to lose enthusiasm no matter how encouragingly Hearthline approached it.

She checked the water. She watched the moving shade. She looked under leaves and compared roots from plants she had already lifted.

The bed was clear. It was watered. It had room.

She was tempted to give it something immediately.

Instead, she carried soil samples to someone who could test them.

The results gave her something less dramatic and more useful than a mysterious garden curse. That bed needed potassium replenished. Another had a different balance. The blueberry patch wanted conditions the vegetable beds did not.

She brought the report home and spread it beside several years of planting notes. In the margins were little sketches of bowls, baskets, and things she had given away.

Tomatoes to the woman with the beans. Onions to the cook down the road. Peppers for a supper she still remembered. Stalks and prunings carried off when she had wanted the garden tidy.

Hearthline looked out at the beds.

“I've been taking things upstairs,” she said.

Thulia opened one eye from her place above the window.

“And I haven't been thinking enough about the basement.”

She had known the soil mattered. She had bought it amendments, worked with it, and complained about it when it stuck to her boots. Yet each successful season had made it easier to look straight past it toward the fruit.

That autumn, she gave the compost heap a proper place.

It had existed before, as a destination behind the shed. Now she could reach it without climbing over spare timber. She kept dry leaves nearby and carried vegetable trimmings out from the kitchen. When the heap became too wet, she changed the mixture. When material was ready, she used it where it suited the bed, alongside the particular amendments the soil report called for.

Compost did not settle every difference among the plots. The blueberries still needed their own care.

Hearthline acquired a small notebook for the heap.

On its cover she wrote **COMPOST**.

Underneath, after some thought, she added **THIS TAKES A WHILE**.

By the following summer, she had opinions about mulch.

She could explain them at some length if someone asked. She could explain them at nearly the same length if someone left an unfamiliar sack beside her gate.

When a neighbor offered a sack of pine sawdust, she carried it straight to the blueberry patch. She had been reading about woody mulches: the moisture they helped conserve, the organic matter they contributed over time. There were ways to improve a bed that would never fit neatly into a list of nutrients.

She worked the fresh mulch into her feeding plan, allowing for the nitrogen its decomposers would need. Then she spread it around the bushes and tucked the empty sack away for another use.

A few dry afternoons later, she parted the mulch and found dampness underneath. She held some between her fingers, pleased with how slowly it was giving up the water.

“You can take your time,” she told it.

She left the watering can where it was.

Across most of the garden, she left the soil structure undisturbed and kept her boots on the paths. A compacted patch where the old shortcut had crossed needed some careful loosening. Elsewhere, there was no reason to dig merely because she owned a fork.

## The useful numbers

The first garden instrument was a thermometer.

Hearthline had instruments in the workshop already, but bringing one outside changed what she noticed. The warm wall and the open bed were not quite the same place. Neither was the sheltered corner where she had put a plant because the pot looked nice there.

A moisture probe followed. Then a way to check acidity, and a little light meter she carried around at different hours, producing a drawing of the garden covered in increasingly thoughtful pencil marks.

Thulia followed these expeditions from the fence posts.

For a few days, Hearthline took readings simply because she could.

Then one of them told her that a visibly wet pot was dry.

She checked the probe, its contact with the growing medium, and the pot itself. By supper she had corrected the reading and washed rather more mud off her hands than the instrument had spared her.

The next page of the notebook began with a question she actually wanted answered.

Why did the pepper by the wall wilt before the other one?

Over several afternoons, she watched the light and checked below the surface. She moved the container. The plant improved. She waited before deciding how much of the improvement belonged to the move and how much to the cooler weather that followed.

Some questions made a pleasant afternoon's work. Others remained penciled in the margin while she went on growing things.

The watering system began with a much simpler desire.

Hearthline wanted to go out for the day without arranging the whole afternoon around a row of pots.

She fitted a line from the rain barrel, a small filter, and a valve she could close by hand. Before trusting the outlets, she let them run into cups. One filled much faster than the others. Another remained nearly empty.

She spent a morning making the water arrive where she intended.

On the first day she used the timer, she stayed home and watched. She checked beneath the mulch afterward, adjusted the duration, and tried again. She learned what changed when the barrel ran low. A clogged outlet gave her another reason to keep inspecting the line.

Eventually, the system gave her what she had wanted.

She went walking.

When she returned, the pots were watered and the garden had passed an ordinary afternoon without her.

She checked once, picked a tomato, and sat down.

## What the husk kept

The corn was her favorite surprise that year.

She had planted a patch of Glass Gem in a sunny space of its own. She knew it could produce beautiful colors. She had seen ears laid out on a table before buying the seed.

Knowing this did very little to prepare her for opening her own.

The first husk folded back around pale yellow, deep violet, and a blue that made her turn the ear toward the window.

The next looked as though someone had spilled amber beads among the purple.

Hearthline laid them side by side.

Then she went back outside for another.

For months, she had measured the conditions around them. The harvest still had something to show her.

Thulia stepped along the porch rail, watching each ear join the others. Hearthline held one up beside the owl's shoulder, comparing a kernel with the small patch of shifting color in her feathers.

Thulia turned away.

The feather changed color.

“That's cheating,” Hearthline said.

There were less successful surprises too.

The blueberries attracted birds with excellent timing. Deer found the tender growth. Hearthline discovered tracks beside the bed one morning and considered the price of the enclosure she would really need.

For now, the blueberry garden would remain a few bushes she could afford to protect as well as she could. It yielded some handfuls of fruit and a thorough acquaintance with the visitors.

She did not enlarge it that year.

There were other things to grow, and the tomatoes remained enthusiastic.

## A small adjustment

Echo came to visit on an afternoon when the watering valve had developed a faint, uneven click.

He heard it before he reached the porch.

Hearthline saw his expression and set a second cup on the table.

“It waters the pots,” she said.

“I didn't say it didn't.”

“The pots are very happy.”

“I'm sure.”

He crouched beside the valve.

“Has it always done that?”

Hearthline put the cups back down.

They closed the water supply, carried the small assembly to the bench, and spent a contented hour discovering why a washer had begun sitting crookedly in its seat. Thulia settled on the chair back, where she could see both the bench and the garden.

When they put the valve back, they checked the outlets again.

The click was gone.

Echo listened for a moment longer, then noticed a different fitting farther down the line.

Hearthline followed his gaze.

She could see an interesting afternoon in that fitting. There might even be a useful improvement in it.

There was also bread on the table, and the tomatoes were already sliced.

“Tomorrow,” she said.

Echo looked at the fitting, then at the table.

“Tomorrow.”

They ate beside the open path. The corn husks rustled where the breeze reached them. Beyond the beds, flowers moved in the patch Hearthline had left for whatever could make good company there.

Later, she collected the vegetable trimmings from preparing supper and carried them behind the shed.

She lifted the compost cover and tipped the bowl.

“Something for downstairs.”

Then she returned to the garden, where her chair was waiting.

## Gardening roots

The Three Sisters scene draws on Indigenous maize–bean–squash growing traditions
in the Americas. The cabin, its neighbor, instruments, and events are fiction;
Hearthline is learning a practice with a history beyond her own garden.

- [USDA National Agricultural Library: The Three Sisters](https://www.nal.usda.gov/collections/stories/three-sisters) — cultural and agricultural context.
- [University of Arkansas Extension: Creating a Three Sisters Garden](https://www.uaex.uada.edu/counties/white/news/horticulture/20210511Creating_Three_Sisters_Garden.aspx) — timing, arrangement, and harvest access.
- [New Mexico State University: Nitrogen Fixation by Legumes](https://pubs.nmsu.edu/_a/A129/) — nitrogen fixation, plant use, decomposition, and harvest removal.
- [Oregon State University: Improving Garden Soils with Organic Matter](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/catalog/ec-1561-improving-garden-soils-organic-matter) — organic matter, water storage, soil structure, and decomposition.
- [Oregon State University: Growing Blueberries in Your Home Garden](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/catalog/ec-1304-growing-blueberries-your-home-garden) — sawdust mulch, moisture conservation, and its place in nutrient management.

These sources support the named gardening principles, not the fictional events
or a universal planting, amendment, or watering prescription.
